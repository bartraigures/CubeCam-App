CubeCam

CubeCam is a camera app for iPhone that finishes each photograph with a film grade and fine grain, while leaving everything Apple's camera does in place.

The iPhone camera is very good at what it does. Deep Fusion, Smart HDR and the Photonic Engine combine several exposures into one clean, detailed picture, but the result has a recognisable digital look, and the usual ways of changing it have drawbacks. Filters tend to flatten the image and lose much of what made it good. Shooting RAW and grading every frame afterwards gives the best results, but it takes time that most pictures don't deserve.

CubeCam takes the photograph Apple's camera has already finished and grades it the way a colourist would, with deep, quiet blacks, highlights that roll off gently, colours that sit together, and a little grain. The grade is visible in the viewfinder as you compose, and it is what gets saved.

    How the looks work

    Each look is graded the way a colourist finishes a film print, rather than designed to look nostalgic on a phone screen: dense blacks, highlights that roll off rather than clip, cool shadows against warm light, and strong colours that print deeper instead of glowing. The ten are CubeCam's own, each built by hand from those few decisions and tested on real photographs. Every one is timed so that a mid-grey card stays exactly where Apple exposed it, which means changing the look never makes the picture darker or brighter. What changes is how the tones are drawn, how the colours relate to each other, and the grain.

    The looks are deliberately subtle. Colour looks are mixed back with Apple's image, and an Amount control fades any of them further still. The three black and white looks are applied in full, since a half-strength black and white is only a washed-out colour photograph.

    All of the looks can be downloaded as .cube files from the Looks page, for use in other apps.

    The grain is a fine speck at every pixel, strongest in the mid-tones and lighter in deep shadow and bright highlights, much as it is on film. At normal viewing size it reads as texture, and when you zoom in it stays crisp instead of breaking up into soft blotches.

    The camera

    The camera screen starts simple, with focal lengths, an exposure scale, the looks and the shutter. Since every photographer works differently, every button, slider and overlay can be moved, removed or added in a layout editor; only the shutter and the way into Settings stay put. The Manual layout, for example, puts a histogram, a level, manual focus and an exposure lock within reach of the thumb.

    The focal lengths run from 13 to 200 mm. Normally the iPhone chooses which of its cameras to use, switching to the main camera in poor light or to the ultra-wide for close work. Tapping the chosen focal length a second time locks CubeCam to that physical lens and stops the switching, which matters when you care how a particular lens draws.

    Tapping the picture focuses and meters on that point. Manual focus has a near-to-far slider with focus peaking, which outlines the sharpest edges in colour as you focus, and the exposure lock holds through lens changes. On an iPhone with Camera Control, pressing the button takes the picture, and a light press brings zoom, exposure, focus and the look under the thumb.

    The app is built to be quick. The camera is already running before the interface has finished drawing, the grade is applied on the graphics chip in the same pass as the viewfinder, and the shutter never waits for a photograph to finish saving.

    Super resolution

    Nobody holds a phone perfectly still, and CubeCam can put that to use. With super resolution switched on, each press of the shutter takes four photographs in quick succession. Between one and the next your hands will have moved by a few pixels, so each photograph has sampled the scene in slightly different places. CubeCam lines the four up, part by part, and merges them into a single picture half as large again in each direction: the 48 megapixel main camera gives a photograph of 108 megapixels.

    It is worth being plain about what this buys. It isn't twice the detail, because Apple's photographs have already been sharpened and cleaned up before CubeCam sees them. What you get is a cleaner picture in which small things hold together better: lettering across a room, hair, the weave of a fabric. Anything that moved during the burst is taken from one photograph alone, so it comes out as it would have in an ordinary shot. The look and the grain go on afterwards, as usual.

    Keeping the original

    Choosing a look is a decision you may want to revisit. By default CubeCam saves each photograph as an edit with Apple's original underneath it, so Revert in the Photos app always takes you back. CubeCam's own viewer uses that original to try a different look on a photograph after the event, to make small adjustments, and to show how the picture was taken. With Apple ProRAW switched on, the RAW file is saved alongside, untouched.

    Editing colours

    Sometimes one colour is wrong: a sky too electric, a jacket that shouts, grass a shade too green. CubeCam's colour editor finds the main colours in a photograph and lets you move them directly. Touch a colour and slide, sideways to turn its hue and up or down to lighten or darken it, and every similar colour in the picture follows while the rest stays put. A colour wheel shows all of them at once, and Borrow colour takes the palette of another photograph you like. The result can be saved into the photograph as a revertible edit, kept as a look of your own, or exported as a .cube file for other apps.

    Photographs from anywhere

    CubeCam isn't only for the pictures it takes. Any photograph in your library can be added to CubeCam's photos from the viewer, and any app that shares photographs can send them to CubeCam from the share sheet, including the apps that bring photographs across from a dedicated camera. Once added, a photograph works like one CubeCam took: try the looks on it, adjust it, edit its colours. Everything is saved as a revertible edit, and a photograph that is already in your library is edited in place rather than copied.

    A copy for somewhere else

    A photograph often has to go somewhere that wants it a particular way. Export a copy, in the viewer, makes a new photograph from the one you have and leaves the first alone. The copy can be full size or smaller, a JPEG or a HEIF, and it can be set whole on a square border in white, off-white or black, which is the simplest way to post an upright or a wide picture where only squares are shown without cropping it.

    Making it your camera

    The iPhone has no setting for a default camera, but you can choose what opens from Camera Control, the Lock Screen, the Action button and Control Center. With CubeCam chosen in those places it opens straight away, even when the phone is locked. The guide explains each one.

    Availability

    CubeCam is for iPhone with iOS 18 or later, and will be on the App Store soon. It is free for a limited time. The guide covers everything it does, and the support page answers the common questions.
